2:T24bb,<p>Texas is the value play of the American job market: the second-largest state economy, no state income tax, and housing costs far below the coastal metros — with salaries that, in tech and energy especially, are closer to coastal levels than most people expect. Austin&#39;s &quot;Silicon Hills,&quot; Houston&#39;s energy corridor, Dallas&#39;s corporate headquarters cluster, and San Antonio&#39;s healthcare and military economy give the state unusual breadth. This guide covers the 2026 numbers: the wage floor, what employers pay, the tax advantage, and what it costs to live there.</p>
<h2>Minimum wage and worker protections in 2026</h2>
<p>Texas has <strong>no state minimum wage law</strong> — the federal <strong>$7.25 per hour</strong> applies, unchanged since 2009. In practice, the market floor is far higher: fast-food and retail employers in the major metros typically advertise $12–$16 per hour because they have to compete for workers. But the legal details matter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tipped workers</strong> can be paid as little as $2.13 per hour in cash wages under the federal tip credit, as long as tips bring total pay to $7.25.</li>
<li><strong>Overtime</strong> follows the federal rule only: 1.5x after 40 hours in a week. Texas has no daily overtime rule and no state-level meal or rest break requirements for adults.</li>
<li><strong>No state pay-transparency law.</strong> Texas employers are not required to publish salary ranges in postings (unlike California, New York, or Washington) — you will need to ask directly and research bands yourself.</li>
<li><strong>At-will employment</strong> is the default, and Texas courts interpret it broadly.</li>
</ul>
<p>The practical takeaway: Texas is an employer-friendly regulatory environment. Your leverage comes from the offer negotiation, not from statutory protections — so research market bands before every interview.</p>
<h2>Major industries and where the jobs are</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Energy — Houston.</strong> The global capital of oil and gas: supermajors, service companies, petrochemicals, and a fast-growing renewables and carbon-capture sector. Energy engineers and geoscientists earn premiums here that few other states match.</li>
<li><strong>Technology — Austin (&quot;Silicon Hills&quot;) and Dallas.</strong> Tesla, Oracle, and Hewlett Packard Enterprise moved headquarters to Texas in recent years; Apple, Google, Meta, and Amazon all run large Austin offices. Dallas–Fort Worth has a deep enterprise-tech and telecom bench (AT&amp;T is headquartered there).</li>
<li><strong>Finance and corporate HQs — Dallas–Fort Worth.</strong> One of the largest concentrations of Fortune 500 headquarters in the country: American Airlines, ExxonMobil, AT&amp;T, McKesson. Corporate finance, accounting, and operations roles are abundant.</li>
<li><strong>Healthcare — Houston, Dallas, San Antonio.</strong> The Texas Medical Center in Houston is the largest medical complex in the world; San Antonio has a major military-medical presence.</li>
<li><strong>Manufacturing and logistics — statewide.</strong> The central location, ports (Houston), and business-friendly regulation make Texas a distribution and manufacturing hub — strong demand for supply-chain, operations, and skilled-trades roles.</li>
<li><strong>Aerospace and defense — Houston, Dallas, San Antonio.</strong> NASA&#39;s Johnson Space Center, plus major defense contractors.</li>
</ul>

<p>The Texas pattern: <strong>headline salaries run 15–30% below coastal equivalents, but purchasing power often runs ahead</strong> because of taxes and housing. A $120,000 offer in Austin can beat a $160,000 offer in San Jose on disposable income.</p>
<h2>The tax picture: zero income tax, with a catch</h2>
<p>Texas charges <strong>no state income tax</strong> — on wages or anything else — and the state constitution makes imposing one very difficult. For a professional earning $120,000, that saves roughly $6,000–$10,000 a year versus a mid-tax state, and far more versus California.</p>
<p>The catch, and it is a real one:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Property taxes are among the highest in the nation.</strong> Texas funds local government heavily through property tax. A homeowner in the Houston or Austin suburbs can pay $8,000–$15,000 a year in property tax on a mid-range home — which offsets a meaningful share of the income-tax savings for buyers. Renters capture more of the benefit, since the tax is embedded in rent at a lower effective rate.</li>
<li><strong>Sales tax</strong> is 6.25% at the state level, up to 8.25% with local add-ons.</li>
</ul>
<p>Net effect: Texas is most advantageous for <strong>high earners who rent or buy modestly</strong>, and somewhat less dramatic for buyers of expensive homes. Run the full comparison for your situation.</p>
<h2>Cost of living: the Texas advantage</h2>
<p>Texas&#39;s typical home value sits around <strong>$301,000</strong> (Zillow, January 2026) — below the national typical value of about $361,000, and less than 40% of California&#39;s. Metro detail:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Austin:</strong> around $421,000 typical — the priciest Texas metro, cooled from its pandemic peak but still the state&#39;s premium market.</li>
<li><strong>Dallas–Fort Worth:</strong> around $358,000 typical; enormous suburban sprawl keeps a wide range of price points available.</li>
<li><strong>Houston:</strong> the most affordable major metro — strong value for money, though flood insurance and hurricane risk are real budget line items in many neighborhoods.</li>
<li><strong>San Antonio:</strong> the most affordable of the big four, with typical values well under $320,000.</li>
</ul>
<p>Renters do well too: one-bedrooms run roughly $1,300–$1,900 in Austin, $1,200–$1,700 in Dallas and Houston, and under $1,200 in San Antonio — roughly half of Bay Area or New York levels. The trade-offs: you will almost certainly need a car (Texas metros are car-dependent), summers are brutally hot (air-conditioning is a real utility bill), and property insurance costs have been climbing.</p>
<h2>Who thrives in Texas</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tech workers</strong> who want strong pay without coastal costs — Austin especially.</li>
<li><strong>Energy professionals</strong> — Houston pays world-leading salaries in oil, gas, and increasingly renewables.</li>
<li><strong>Healthcare workers</strong> — huge systems, steady demand, reasonable costs.</li>
<li><strong>Remote workers</strong> — no income tax on your paycheck regardless of where your employer sits.</li>
<li><strong>Anyone comparing offers across states</strong> — Texas almost always wins the purchasing-power math.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Who should think twice</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Workers counting on strong labor protections</strong> — Texas is at-will, low-regulation, and has no state pay-transparency or paid-leave mandates.</li>
<li><strong>Public-school teachers</strong> — pay lags the national average and the political environment around education funding is contentious.</li>
<li><strong>Car-free lifestylists</strong> — outside central Austin, public transit is thin.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Next steps</h2>
<ol>
<li>Because Texas has no pay-transparency law, research every role&#39;s band on levels.fyi and Glassdoor <em>before</em> the interview — you will not get the range from the posting.</li>
<li>Compare Texas offers against coastal ones on an after-tax, after-housing basis; the Texas number usually wins at 70–80% of the coastal number.</li>
<li>If buying, model property tax explicitly — get the actual rate for the specific county and school district, not the state average.</li>
<li>Applying from abroad? Read our H-1B guide — Texas employers are among the country&#39;s biggest H-1B sponsors, especially in tech and healthcare.</li>
</ol>
