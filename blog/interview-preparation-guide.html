Interview Preparation for US Roles: From Video Setup to Salary Negotiation

By Editorial Team2026-10-18Application Prepglobal

International interviews are a different game

Interviewing for a role in New York, Austin, or San Francisco while sitting in Lahore or Karachi adds layers that local candidates never deal with: video-call logistics across an 8–10 hour time difference, proving you can work with a US-based team, and negotiating a salary in dollars you do not spend. Employers are not just assessing your skills — they are assessing risk. Every answer you give should quietly reduce their perceived risk of hiring someone across borders.

The video interview setup (get this right first)

Most international hiring starts with one or two video interviews. A surprising number of strong candidates lose at this stage for entirely avoidable technical reasons.

Your environment:

  • Lighting: face a window or put a lamp in front of you, not behind you. Backlighting turns you into a silhouette.
  • Background: plain wall or a tidy room. Virtual backgrounds are acceptable but a real, neutral background reads as more professional.
  • Camera: eye level. Stack your laptop on books if needed. Looking down at a camera reads as disengaged.
  • Audio: this matters more than video. Use earphones with a microphone or a basic headset — laptop mics pick up every echo and fan noise. Test with a friend beforehand.
  • Connection: use the most stable internet you have. If your home connection drops, have a mobile hotspot ready as backup. Close bandwidth-heavy apps before the call.
  • Power: plug in. A dying laptop mid-interview is a story you do not want.

Logistics across time zones:

  • Confirm the time zone explicitly in your reply: "Looking forward to speaking at 10 AM ET / 7 PM PKT on Tuesday." Never assume.
  • Join 5–10 minutes early. For international calls, early also gives you buffer if the link or platform is unfamiliar.
  • Keep your phone on silent and notifications off — a WhatsApp ping mid-answer is distracting.
  • Have water nearby, a notepad, the job description printed or open, and your resume in front of you. This is an open-book test; use it.

On camera presence:

  • Look into the camera when making key points, not at the interviewer's video feed. It reads as eye contact.
  • Speak slightly slower than feels natural — compression and accents compound over video.
  • Pause before answering hard questions. Two seconds of silence reads as thoughtful; rambling reads as nervous.

Common questions and the frameworks that answer them

Interviewers at international companies ask predictable questions. What separates strong candidates is structure, not memorized scripts.

"Tell me about yourself." Use the Present–Past–Future framework, in 90 seconds: where you are now (role, scope), how you got here (2–3 relevant beats), where you are headed (why this role, why this move). End by connecting to the role. Never recite your resume chronologically and never start with your childhood.

"Why do you want to work for us / relocate?" Employers hiring across borders are screening for flight risk. Be specific about the company — cite a product, a market position, a project — and frame relocation as a deliberate career decision, not an escape. "I've spent four years in fintech in Pakistan; your expansion into US payments is exactly the scale I want to work at next" beats "I've always wanted to live in America."

"Tell me about a time you..." (behavioral questions) Use the STAR method: Situation (one line of context), Task (what was required of you), Action (what you specifically did — not "we"), Result (quantified outcome). Then stop. The most common failure is a five-minute story with no result. Prepare 6–8 STAR stories covering: a difficult project, a conflict with a colleague, a failure and what you learned, leading without authority, working under a tight deadline, and dealing with ambiguity. These six cover roughly 80% of behavioral questions.

"What is your greatest weakness?" Name a real, professional weakness — not a disguised strength ("I work too hard"). Then show what you are doing about it: "Early in my career I avoided pushing back on unrealistic deadlines, which led to burnout on one project. Now I negotiate scope explicitly at kickoff — I built a simple estimation checklist my team still uses." Honest, specific, resolved.

"Why should we hire you over local candidates?" This is the expat-tax question, asked directly or indirectly. Answer with what you uniquely bring: niche expertise, experience in high-growth or cost-constrained environments, multilingual ability, or a track record of ramping fast in new contexts. Do not apologize for being remote or foreign — sell the arbitrage: "Teams in high-growth markets learn to do more with less. I bring that operating discipline plus the exact stack you run on."

"Do you have questions for us?" Always have three ready. Good ones reveal genuine thinking: "What does success look like in the first 90 days?", "What is the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?", "How is performance reviewed here?" Never ask about salary, holidays, or perks in the first interview — and never say "no, I'm good."

Questions they may ask about your cross-border situation

Expect direct questions about logistics, and answer them like someone who has already thought it through:

  • Work authorization / sponsorship: know your answer cold. "I would need H-1B sponsorship" or "I'm a US citizen — no sponsorship needed." Hesitation here reads as unpreparedness.
  • Relocation timeline: "I can relocate within 30 days of a signed offer; my passport is current and I can begin the visa process immediately." Concrete beats vague.
  • Remote work overlap: if the role is remote, state your working hours explicitly: "I work 7 PM – 4 AM PKT, which gives full overlap with US East Coast working hours." Employers worry about timezone drag — remove the worry with specifics.
  • Salary expectations in their currency: research before the call (see below). Never say "whatever you offer is fine."

Salary negotiation basics

For many South Asian candidates, this is the hardest part — and the most expensive to get wrong. A few principles from people who have sat on the hiring side:

1. Never give a number first if you can avoid it. When asked for expectations early, redirect: "I'd like to understand the full scope of the role first — what's the budgeted range for this position?" Many US employers are now required to disclose pay ranges in job postings (several states, including California, New York, and Washington, mandate this) — use those published ranges. If pressed, give a researched range, not a single figure, and anchor it to market data: "Based on my research for this role in Austin, I'm looking at $95,000–$120,000 base, depending on the total package."

2. Research the actual market, not your home market. US salaries follow local bands that vary enormously by state and metro — a software engineer in San Francisco can earn 40%+ more than one in Miami. Check levels.fyi and Glassdoor for tech roles, and state salary surveys for others. A common and costly mistake is converting your Pakistani salary and adding a margin — US offers do not work that way, and you will underprice yourself by 30–50%.

3. Negotiate the total package, not just the base. In the US, health insurance premiums, 401(k) matching, bonuses, equity/RSUs, and paid time off can be worth 20–40% on top of base salary. Startups may offer meaningful equity; established firms may offer strong bonuses. Get the full breakdown in writing before deciding.

4. Understand the tax picture. There is no single US tax rate — federal income tax applies everywhere, then state income tax ranges from zero (Texas, Florida, Washington) to a top marginal rate above 13% (California). A $130,000 offer in Texas can leave you with more take-home pay than a $160,000 offer in California. Compare net, not gross. Also confirm who pays for visa costs, relocation, and initial accommodation — legitimate US employers hiring internationally typically cover these.

5. Do not accept on the call. "Thank you — I'm very excited about this. Can I have until Friday to review the written offer?" is always acceptable. Employers expect it. Use the time to compare the full package and, if you have competing processes, to accelerate them.

6. Get everything in writing. Verbal promises about bonuses, remote flexibility, or relocation support are worth nothing. The written offer letter and contract are the only things that count.

After the interview

  • Send a short thank-you email within 24 hours: thank them for their time, reference one specific thing discussed, restate your enthusiasm in one line. Keep it under 100 words.
  • If they gave you a timeline ("we'll decide by next Friday"), follow up once, politely, the day after it passes. Then move on mentally — chasing never helps.
  • Keep other processes warm. Your negotiating power comes from options, not from need.

Next steps

  1. Do a full tech rehearsal this week: lighting, audio, backup internet, and a test call with a friend.
  2. Write out 6 STAR stories and rehearse each aloud until it fits in two minutes.
  3. Research salary bands for your next three target roles in their local currency — before any interview, not during.
  4. Prepare your logistics answers (sponsorship, relocation timeline, timezone overlap) as one-liners you can deliver without hesitation.
