2:T20d2,<h2>Do cover letters still matter?</h2>
<p>Yes — with a caveat. Nobody reads a generic cover letter. But for competitive roles, career changes, and international applications, a tailored cover letter is often the difference between the shortlist and the bin. Many US hiring managers still expect one for senior and customer-facing roles, and surveys of US hiring managers consistently show a majority say a tailored cover letter improves a candidate&#39;s chances. For applicants from Pakistan and South Asia, the cover letter does something your resume cannot — it explains <em>why this move, why this company, why now</em>, which is exactly what a hiring manager wonders when they see a cross-border application.</p>
<p>The rule is simple: one page maximum, three paragraphs, tailored every single time. A recycled cover letter is worse than none at all — recruiters can tell in the first line.</p>
<h2>The 3-paragraph structure</h2>
<p><strong>Paragraph 1: Why this role, specifically (3–4 lines).</strong>
Name the exact role and where you saw it, then say in one or two sentences why it fits your trajectory. Specificity is everything — mention something real about the company.</p>
<p><strong>Paragraph 2: Why you — the evidence (the longest paragraph, 6–8 lines).</strong>
Pick two or three achievements resume that map directly onto the job description&#39;s top requirements. Quantify them. This is not a summary of your resume; it is a curated argument: <em>here is the proof I can do this job.</em></p>
<p><strong>Paragraph 3: Why now, and the close (3–4 lines).</strong>
Address the cross-border question directly — relocation readiness, sponsorship needs, notice period, timezone overlap for remote roles. End with a clear, confident call to action. Thank them for their consideration.</p>
<p>Then sign off with your name, phone, and email.</p>
<h2>Good lines vs bad lines</h2>
<p>The difference between a cover letter that gets read and one that gets skimmed is almost entirely in the specificity of the lines. Compare:</p>
<p><strong>Opening lines</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Bad: &quot;I am writing to apply for the position advertised on your website. I believe I would be a great fit for your company.&quot;</li>
<li>Good: &quot;I&#39;m applying for the Senior QA Engineer role — your team&#39;s migration to Playwright-based testing is exactly the work I&#39;ve led for the last two years at two fintech companies.&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>The bad version could be sent to any company for any role. The good version proves you researched this one.</p>
<p><strong>Evidence lines</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Bad: &quot;I have excellent communication skills and am a hard worker who thrives under pressure.&quot;</li>
<li>Good: &quot;I led a team of six support engineers handling 400+ tickets a month, cutting average resolution time from 72 to 28 hours while lifting CSAT from 3.8 to 4.6.&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>The bad version makes claims. The good version provides evidence. Hiring managers believe evidence.</p>
<p><strong>Motivation lines</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Bad: &quot;I have always dreamed of working abroad and believe your company would be a great opportunity for my career growth.&quot;</li>
<li>Good: &quot;After four years building payments products for the Pakistani market, I want to work at the scale your US expansion represents — and my experience with low-bandwidth, mobile-first users maps directly onto your customer base.&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>The bad version is about what the company can do for you. The good version is about what you bring to them.</p>
<p><strong>Closing lines</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Bad: &quot;I hope to hear from you soon. Thank you for your time.&quot;</li>
<li>Good: &quot;I&#39;m based in Karachi and can relocate within 30 days of an offer; my passport is current. I&#39;d welcome the chance to discuss how my experience fits your roadmap — I&#39;m available for a call at your convenience, including 7–11 PM PKT for full overlap with US East Coast hours.&quot;</li>
</ul>
<p>The bad version ends with a whimper. The good version removes the hiring manager&#39;s logistical worries and makes the next step easy.</p>
<h2>Tailoring: the 20-minute method</h2>
<p>&quot;I don&#39;t have time to tailor every application&quot; is the reason most cover letters fail. You do not need an hour. You need 20 focused minutes:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Read the job description and highlight the top 3 requirements</strong> (5 min). These are usually the first three bullets or the &quot;must-have&quot; section.</li>
<li><strong>Match one achievement to each requirement</strong> from your experience (10 min). Pull the numbers from your resume.</li>
<li><strong>Rewrite paragraph 1 and the closing logistics line</strong> for this specific company and role (5 min). One genuine sentence about the company — a product launch, a market move, a value they state — proves this letter was written today, for them.</li>
</ol>
<p>Keep a document of your best 8–10 achievement lines with numbers, so you are assembling rather than writing from scratch each time.</p>
<h2>The cross-border paragraph: say it plainly</h2>
<p>If you are applying from Pakistan or South Asia, do not hide the logistics — address them confidently in the closing paragraph:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sponsorship:</strong> &quot;I would require H-1B visa sponsorship&quot; — stated plainly, early. Or, if you do not need it: &quot;I hold [X status] and require no sponsorship.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Relocation:</strong> &quot;I&#39;m prepared to relocate within 30 days of a signed offer.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Remote:</strong> &quot;I work 7 PM–4 AM PKT, giving full overlap with US East Coast working hours.&quot;</li>
<li><strong>Documents:</strong> &quot;My credentials are ready for verification, and I can start the visa process immediately after an offer&quot; — this small line signals seriousness to US employers.</li>
</ul>
<p>Hiring managers&#39; biggest fear with international candidates is uncertainty. Every concrete logistical fact you provide shrinks that fear.</p>
<h2>Mistakes that kill cover letters</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>&quot;To whom it may concern&quot;</strong> — find the hiring manager&#39;s name on LinkedIn or the company site. If you genuinely cannot, &quot;Dear Hiring Manager&quot; is acceptable.</li>
<li><strong>Repeating your resume</strong> — the letter argues; the resume documents. Never copy bullets across.</li>
<li><strong>Flattery without substance</strong> — &quot;your esteemed organization&quot; and &quot;world-renowned company&quot; read as template filler. One specific fact about the company beats a paragraph of praise.</li>
<li><strong>Apologizing for your background</strong> — never write &quot;although I lack US experience&quot; or &quot;despite being based in Pakistan&quot;. Frame your background as an asset or say nothing.</li>
<li><strong>Typos and wrong company names</strong> — the fastest way to the bin. Read it aloud once before sending; your ear catches what your eye misses.</li>
<li><strong>Over one page</strong> — if it does not fit on one page, cut paragraph 2. Ruthlessly.</li>
<li><strong>US spelling</strong> — for US employers, use American spelling throughout: &quot;organized&quot;, &quot;specialized&quot;, &quot;center&quot;. Consistency signals attention to detail.</li>
</ul>
<h2>A note on AI-written cover letters</h2>
<p>Recruiters now see hundreds of AI-generated cover letters a week and have developed a reliable ear for them: the same polished-but-empty phrasing, the same three generic strengths. Using AI to draft is fine; sending the draft untouched is not. The 20-minute tailoring method above is precisely what makes a letter unmistakably human — specific company facts, real numbers from your history, and your actual voice.</p>
<h2>Next steps</h2>
<ol>
<li>Build your achievement bank: 8–10 lines from your career, each with a number, saved in one document.</li>
<li>Write one master cover letter in the 3-paragraph structure, with the company-specific and logistics lines left as blanks to fill per application.</li>
<li>For your next application, run the 20-minute tailoring method — then read the letter aloud once before sending.</li>
<li>Track which tailored letters get responses; within a month you will see the pattern in your own data.</li>
</ol>
