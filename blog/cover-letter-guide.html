Cover Letters That Get Read: The 3-Paragraph Structure That Works

By Editorial Team2026-10-18Application Prepglobal

Do cover letters still matter?

Yes — with a caveat. Nobody reads a generic cover letter. But for competitive roles, career changes, and international applications, a tailored cover letter is often the difference between the shortlist and the bin. Many US hiring managers still expect one for senior and customer-facing roles, and surveys of US hiring managers consistently show a majority say a tailored cover letter improves a candidate's chances. For applicants from Pakistan and South Asia, the cover letter does something your resume cannot — it explains why this move, why this company, why now, which is exactly what a hiring manager wonders when they see a cross-border application.

The rule is simple: one page maximum, three paragraphs, tailored every single time. A recycled cover letter is worse than none at all — recruiters can tell in the first line.

The 3-paragraph structure

Paragraph 1: Why this role, specifically (3–4 lines). Name the exact role and where you saw it, then say in one or two sentences why it fits your trajectory. Specificity is everything — mention something real about the company.

Paragraph 2: Why you — the evidence (the longest paragraph, 6–8 lines). Pick two or three achievements resume that map directly onto the job description's top requirements. Quantify them. This is not a summary of your resume; it is a curated argument: here is the proof I can do this job.

Paragraph 3: Why now, and the close (3–4 lines). Address the cross-border question directly — relocation readiness, sponsorship needs, notice period, timezone overlap for remote roles. End with a clear, confident call to action. Thank them for their consideration.

Then sign off with your name, phone, and email.

Good lines vs bad lines

The difference between a cover letter that gets read and one that gets skimmed is almost entirely in the specificity of the lines. Compare:

Opening lines

  • Bad: "I am writing to apply for the position advertised on your website. I believe I would be a great fit for your company."
  • Good: "I'm applying for the Senior QA Engineer role — your team's migration to Playwright-based testing is exactly the work I've led for the last two years at two fintech companies."

The bad version could be sent to any company for any role. The good version proves you researched this one.

Evidence lines

  • Bad: "I have excellent communication skills and am a hard worker who thrives under pressure."
  • Good: "I led a team of six support engineers handling 400+ tickets a month, cutting average resolution time from 72 to 28 hours while lifting CSAT from 3.8 to 4.6."

The bad version makes claims. The good version provides evidence. Hiring managers believe evidence.

Motivation lines

  • Bad: "I have always dreamed of working abroad and believe your company would be a great opportunity for my career growth."
  • Good: "After four years building payments products for the Pakistani market, I want to work at the scale your US expansion represents — and my experience with low-bandwidth, mobile-first users maps directly onto your customer base."

The bad version is about what the company can do for you. The good version is about what you bring to them.

Closing lines

  • Bad: "I hope to hear from you soon. Thank you for your time."
  • Good: "I'm based in Karachi and can relocate within 30 days of an offer; my passport is current. I'd welcome the chance to discuss how my experience fits your roadmap — I'm available for a call at your convenience, including 7–11 PM PKT for full overlap with US East Coast hours."

The bad version ends with a whimper. The good version removes the hiring manager's logistical worries and makes the next step easy.

Tailoring: the 20-minute method

"I don't have time to tailor every application" is the reason most cover letters fail. You do not need an hour. You need 20 focused minutes:

  1. Read the job description and highlight the top 3 requirements (5 min). These are usually the first three bullets or the "must-have" section.
  2. Match one achievement to each requirement from your experience (10 min). Pull the numbers from your resume.
  3. Rewrite paragraph 1 and the closing logistics line for this specific company and role (5 min). One genuine sentence about the company — a product launch, a market move, a value they state — proves this letter was written today, for them.

Keep a document of your best 8–10 achievement lines with numbers, so you are assembling rather than writing from scratch each time.

The cross-border paragraph: say it plainly

If you are applying from Pakistan or South Asia, do not hide the logistics — address them confidently in the closing paragraph:

  • Sponsorship: "I would require H-1B visa sponsorship" — stated plainly, early. Or, if you do not need it: "I hold [X status] and require no sponsorship."
  • Relocation: "I'm prepared to relocate within 30 days of a signed offer."
  • Remote: "I work 7 PM–4 AM PKT, giving full overlap with US East Coast working hours."
  • Documents: "My credentials are ready for verification, and I can start the visa process immediately after an offer" — this small line signals seriousness to US employers.

Hiring managers' biggest fear with international candidates is uncertainty. Every concrete logistical fact you provide shrinks that fear.

Mistakes that kill cover letters

  • "To whom it may concern" — find the hiring manager's name on LinkedIn or the company site. If you genuinely cannot, "Dear Hiring Manager" is acceptable.
  • Repeating your resume — the letter argues; the resume documents. Never copy bullets across.
  • Flattery without substance — "your esteemed organization" and "world-renowned company" read as template filler. One specific fact about the company beats a paragraph of praise.
  • Apologizing for your background — never write "although I lack US experience" or "despite being based in Pakistan". Frame your background as an asset or say nothing.
  • Typos and wrong company names — the fastest way to the bin. Read it aloud once before sending; your ear catches what your eye misses.
  • Over one page — if it does not fit on one page, cut paragraph 2. Ruthlessly.
  • US spelling — for US employers, use American spelling throughout: "organized", "specialized", "center". Consistency signals attention to detail.

A note on AI-written cover letters

Recruiters now see hundreds of AI-generated cover letters a week and have developed a reliable ear for them: the same polished-but-empty phrasing, the same three generic strengths. Using AI to draft is fine; sending the draft untouched is not. The 20-minute tailoring method above is precisely what makes a letter unmistakably human — specific company facts, real numbers from your history, and your actual voice.

Next steps

  1. Build your achievement bank: 8–10 lines from your career, each with a number, saved in one document.
  2. Write one master cover letter in the 3-paragraph structure, with the company-specific and logistics lines left as blanks to fill per application.
  3. For your next application, run the 20-minute tailoring method — then read the letter aloud once before sending.
  4. Track which tailored letters get responses; within a month you will see the pattern in your own data.
