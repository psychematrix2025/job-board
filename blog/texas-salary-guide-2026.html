Texas Salary Guide 2026: Minimum Wage, Pay by Role, and Cost of Living

By Editorial Team2026-10-19Salary Guidesusa

Texas is the value play of the American job market: the second-largest state economy, no state income tax, and housing costs far below the coastal metros — with salaries that, in tech and energy especially, are closer to coastal levels than most people expect. Austin's "Silicon Hills," Houston's energy corridor, Dallas's corporate headquarters cluster, and San Antonio's healthcare and military economy give the state unusual breadth. This guide covers the 2026 numbers: the wage floor, what employers pay, the tax advantage, and what it costs to live there.

Minimum wage and worker protections in 2026

Texas has no state minimum wage law — the federal $7.25 per hour applies, unchanged since 2009. In practice, the market floor is far higher: fast-food and retail employers in the major metros typically advertise $12–$16 per hour because they have to compete for workers. But the legal details matter:

  • Tipped workers can be paid as little as $2.13 per hour in cash wages under the federal tip credit, as long as tips bring total pay to $7.25.
  • Overtime follows the federal rule only: 1.5x after 40 hours in a week. Texas has no daily overtime rule and no state-level meal or rest break requirements for adults.
  • No state pay-transparency law. Texas employers are not required to publish salary ranges in postings (unlike California, New York, or Washington) — you will need to ask directly and research bands yourself.
  • At-will employment is the default, and Texas courts interpret it broadly.

The practical takeaway: Texas is an employer-friendly regulatory environment. Your leverage comes from the offer negotiation, not from statutory protections — so research market bands before every interview.

Major industries and where the jobs are

  • Energy — Houston. The global capital of oil and gas: supermajors, service companies, petrochemicals, and a fast-growing renewables and carbon-capture sector. Energy engineers and geoscientists earn premiums here that few other states match.
  • Technology — Austin ("Silicon Hills") and Dallas. Tesla, Oracle, and Hewlett Packard Enterprise moved headquarters to Texas in recent years; Apple, Google, Meta, and Amazon all run large Austin offices. Dallas–Fort Worth has a deep enterprise-tech and telecom bench (AT&T is headquartered there).
  • Finance and corporate HQs — Dallas–Fort Worth. One of the largest concentrations of Fortune 500 headquarters in the country: American Airlines, ExxonMobil, AT&T, McKesson. Corporate finance, accounting, and operations roles are abundant.
  • Healthcare — Houston, Dallas, San Antonio. The Texas Medical Center in Houston is the largest medical complex in the world; San Antonio has a major military-medical presence.
  • Manufacturing and logistics — statewide. The central location, ports (Houston), and business-friendly regulation make Texas a distribution and manufacturing hub — strong demand for supply-chain, operations, and skilled-trades roles.
  • Aerospace and defense — Houston, Dallas, San Antonio. NASA's Johnson Space Center, plus major defense contractors.

The Texas pattern: headline salaries run 15–30% below coastal equivalents, but purchasing power often runs ahead because of taxes and housing. A $120,000 offer in Austin can beat a $160,000 offer in San Jose on disposable income.

The tax picture: zero income tax, with a catch

Texas charges no state income tax — on wages or anything else — and the state constitution makes imposing one very difficult. For a professional earning $120,000, that saves roughly $6,000–$10,000 a year versus a mid-tax state, and far more versus California.

The catch, and it is a real one:

  • Property taxes are among the highest in the nation. Texas funds local government heavily through property tax. A homeowner in the Houston or Austin suburbs can pay $8,000–$15,000 a year in property tax on a mid-range home — which offsets a meaningful share of the income-tax savings for buyers. Renters capture more of the benefit, since the tax is embedded in rent at a lower effective rate.
  • Sales tax is 6.25% at the state level, up to 8.25% with local add-ons.

Net effect: Texas is most advantageous for high earners who rent or buy modestly, and somewhat less dramatic for buyers of expensive homes. Run the full comparison for your situation.

Cost of living: the Texas advantage

Texas's typical home value sits around $301,000 (Zillow, January 2026) — below the national typical value of about $361,000, and less than 40% of California's. Metro detail:

  • Austin: around $421,000 typical — the priciest Texas metro, cooled from its pandemic peak but still the state's premium market.
  • Dallas–Fort Worth: around $358,000 typical; enormous suburban sprawl keeps a wide range of price points available.
  • Houston: the most affordable major metro — strong value for money, though flood insurance and hurricane risk are real budget line items in many neighborhoods.
  • San Antonio: the most affordable of the big four, with typical values well under $320,000.

Renters do well too: one-bedrooms run roughly $1,300–$1,900 in Austin, $1,200–$1,700 in Dallas and Houston, and under $1,200 in San Antonio — roughly half of Bay Area or New York levels. The trade-offs: you will almost certainly need a car (Texas metros are car-dependent), summers are brutally hot (air-conditioning is a real utility bill), and property insurance costs have been climbing.

Who thrives in Texas

  • Tech workers who want strong pay without coastal costs — Austin especially.
  • Energy professionals — Houston pays world-leading salaries in oil, gas, and increasingly renewables.
  • Healthcare workers — huge systems, steady demand, reasonable costs.
  • Remote workers — no income tax on your paycheck regardless of where your employer sits.
  • Anyone comparing offers across states — Texas almost always wins the purchasing-power math.

Who should think twice

  • Workers counting on strong labor protections — Texas is at-will, low-regulation, and has no state pay-transparency or paid-leave mandates.
  • Public-school teachers — pay lags the national average and the political environment around education funding is contentious.
  • Car-free lifestylists — outside central Austin, public transit is thin.

Next steps

  1. Because Texas has no pay-transparency law, research every role's band on levels.fyi and Glassdoor before the interview — you will not get the range from the posting.
  2. Compare Texas offers against coastal ones on an after-tax, after-housing basis; the Texas number usually wins at 70–80% of the coastal number.
  3. If buying, model property tax explicitly — get the actual rate for the specific county and school district, not the state average.
  4. Applying from abroad? Read our H-1B guide — Texas employers are among the country's biggest H-1B sponsors, especially in tech and healthcare.
