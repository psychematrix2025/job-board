US Job Scams: How to Spot Them and Protect Yourself

By Editorial Team2026-10-18Application Prepusa

Why this guide exists

Every year, thousands of Pakistani job seekers lose money — sometimes their family's savings — to US job fraud. The pattern is devastatingly consistent: an attractive offer, a demand for upfront payment, then silence. The Bureau of Emigration and Overseas Employment (BEOE) issues fresh advisories about it every year, most recently warning specifically against fake offers demanding advance payments and against traveling on visit visas with promises of jobs on arrival.

Scammers succeed because they exploit hope and urgency. This guide gives you a clear-eyed checklist: the red flags, how to verify anyone who offers you work abroad, and exactly what to do if you have already been targeted.

The red flags: any one of these is enough to walk away

1. They ask you to pay for the job. This is the big one, and the BEOE states it plainly: any overseas job offer that requires you to pay — visa fees, processing charges, recruitment costs, "security deposits" — should be treated as a serious red flag. Legitimate, internationally recognized employers bear the costs of recruitment, visa processing, and placement. They do not ask candidates for advance payments. The moment money flows from you to them before you have a verified job, stop.

2. The offer is unrealistically good. A "warehouse worker wanted in Texas, $7,000/month, no experience needed, US work visa in 7 days" is not a job — it is bait. Compare every offer against real US market salaries (check our US salary guides or levels.fyi for tech roles). Scammers deliberately pitch above market to short-circuit your skepticism. Real US employers do not hire warehouse or driving staff sight-unseen from Pakistan — low-skill US work visas for foreigners are extremely restricted.

3. They promise a job on a visit or tourist visa. The BEOE issued a specific advisory on this in September 2026: do not trust agents who promise employment after you arrive on a visit visa, or who claim a visit visa can easily be converted into a work visa. Visit visas generally do not permit employment, no agent can legally guarantee a conversion, and working illegally exposes you to arrest, fines, deportation, and entry bans — plus exploitation like unpaid wages and passport confiscation.

4. They sell you a US "work permit" or fast-track green card. There is no such thing as buying a US work permit. US work visas (H-1B, L-1, O-1) are petition-based — an employer files for you, waits months, and pays thousands in government fees. Anyone offering to "get you an H-1B" or "process your green card" for a flat fee you pay is running a scam or a visa mill that can permanently damage your immigration record. Similarly, the Diversity Visa (green card lottery) is real — but entries are free and filed only at dvprogram.state.gov during the entry period each year. Any email saying you "won" the lottery and must pay a processing fee to claim it is fraud; the US State Department warns that it never notifies selectees by email.

5. A travel agency offers you a job. Travel agencies are legally authorized only for travel services — tickets, hotel bookings. They have no legal role in overseas recruitment or work visas. The BEOE's position is unambiguous: do not pay any travel agency for employment or work visas. Recruitment must go through licensed channels.

6. No verifiable license or identity. A legitimate overseas recruiter in Pakistan must be a licensed Overseas Employment Promoter (OEP). If they refuse to share their OEP license number, cannot produce it, or get evasive when you ask to verify — that is your answer. Similarly, if the entire "recruitment process" happens over WhatsApp with someone you have never met, at an office you have never seen, treat it as fraud until proven otherwise.

7. Pressure and secrecy. "Pay today or the visa slot goes to someone else." "Don't tell anyone about this opportunity." Legitimate hiring does not work on countdown timers, and legitimate recruiters welcome verification — they encourage it.

8. They want your original passport or blank signed papers. Never hand over your original passport to an agent, and never sign blank forms, blank sheets, or incomplete contracts. Both are tools for coercion and fraud.

9. No written documentation. A real offer comes with a signed offer letter on company letterhead, a detailed employment contract, and verifiable visa documents — plus official receipts for any legitimate payment. Verbal promises, screenshots of "visa approvals," and emotional assurances are worth nothing.

How to verify an employer or recruiter

Run these checks before you pay anything, sign anything, or travel:

Step 1: Check the recruiter's license. Visit the BEOE website (beoe.gov.pk) and search their list of licensed Overseas Employment Promoters. Confirm the agency's name, license number, and that the license is current. If they are not on the list, they are operating illegally. Do not proceed.

Step 2: Verify the job itself. The BEOE's Foreign Jobs Portal lets you check advertised foreign jobs and the permissions behind them. Ask the recruiter for the job's Permission Number and cross-check it. A licensed recruiter with a real mandate will provide this without hesitation.

Step 3: Verify the foreign employer independently. Look up the company: official website (check the domain registration and contact details), LinkedIn company page with real employees, business registration in the destination country, and reviews from current or former staff. Call the company's publicly listed phone number — not the number the agent gave you — and ask to confirm the opening and the recruitment arrangement. Scammers impersonate real companies; the company's own switchboard is the way to catch it.

Step 4: Visit the recruiter's office. Go in person during business hours. Confirm the office exists at the stated address, looks like a real business, and displays its licensing. Fake addresses are one of the most common scam tells.

Step 5: Read the contract before signing. The Foreign Service Agreement should spell out the job title, salary, working hours, accommodation, and who pays for what. Have someone experienced read it with you if the language is dense. Never sign a contract you have not fully read, in a language you understand.

Step 6: Complete Protector registration. Before departing on an employment visa, register with the Protector of Emigrants (E-Protector online). This puts your contract on government record and activates state protection and insurance mechanisms. It is mandatory — and it is also a final verification checkpoint, because the process itself surfaces irregularities.

Common scam scripts to recognize

  • "Pay the visa fee to this account and your US work permit arrives in 10 days." Real visa fees are paid by the employer through official channels, with receipts.
  • "This warehouse job in New Jersey needs a $2,500 training deposit." There is no such deposit in legitimate US hiring.
  • "You've won the green card lottery! Just pay the processing fee." The Diversity Visa program is free to enter; official results are checked only on dvprogram.state.gov. Payment requests mean fraud.
  • "Send a copy of your passport and degree for verification, plus a small processing fee." Document copies plus a fee request is a classic opener — the fee is the entire point.
  • "You've been selected! Just pay for the medical and the SSN card in advance." Selection without an interview, followed by a payment request, is textbook fraud. Note: a Social Security number is only issued after you are authorized to work — nobody can sell you one in advance.
  • "My contact at the US embassy can fast-track your visa." Nobody fast-tracks visas. US consular processing follows its own timeline and its own official fees.

If you have been scammed

Act quickly — speed matters for recovery and for stopping the same scammer from targeting others:

  1. Stop all payments immediately and cut contact. Do not pay "one more fee" to unlock what you already paid — that is the sunk-cost trap the scam is designed around.
  2. Preserve everything: screenshots of chats, payment receipts and transfer records, the agent's phone numbers, office address, advertisements, and any documents they gave you. Do not delete anything in anger.
  3. Report to the Federal Investigation Agency (FIA). The BEOE specifically directs victims and witnesses to report to the FIA so legal action can be taken. File a written complaint with your evidence attached.
  4. Report to the BEOE as well, particularly if a licensed OEP is involved — license violations can get an agency shut down.
  5. Warn others. Report the fraudulent social media accounts and phone numbers; post a factual account in job-seeker communities so the same script does not catch the next person.
  6. Do not travel on the scammer's instructions. If they tell you to fly out on a visit visa while they "sort the paperwork," refuse. Arriving in a foreign country at a scammer's direction is how exploitation begins.

The mindset that keeps you safe

Legitimate overseas employment has a texture: paperwork, waiting, verifiable institutions, and an employer who spends money on you — not the other way around. Anything that inverts that flow, rushes you past verification, or asks you to trust a stranger with your savings deserves your suspicion, not your hope. Verify first, pay never, and travel only on documents you have confirmed through official channels.

Next steps

  1. Before engaging any recruiter, bookmark the BEOE's OEP list and Foreign Jobs Portal — verification takes ten minutes and saves lakhs.
  2. Apply the red-flag checklist to every offer you currently have in play; kill any that fail even one check.
  3. Complete your document attestation through the official chain (HEC/IBCC → MOFA → embassy) rather than through anyone offering shortcuts.
  4. Share this guide with one person in your circle who is also job hunting abroad — scammers rely on victims staying quiet.
