How to Get a US Job from Abroad: The Practical Playbook

By Editorial Team2026-10-19Application Prepusa

Getting hired by a US employer while living in another country is entirely possible — thousands of people do it every year — but it does not work by accident. The winners follow a system: they pick the right visa lane, target the right employers, present an American-format application, and run the search like a project. This is that system, step by step.

Step 1: Choose your lane before you apply anywhere

Every US employment route runs through an employer. There are four realistic lanes from abroad — pick one primary and one backup, because each demands a different strategy:

Lane A — Direct sponsorship (H-1B, O-1). A US employer petitions for your work visa. Best for mid-to-senior specialists in fields with real talent shortages. The H-1B runs on an annual cycle (employer registration in March, start date October 1) with a weighted lottery that now favors higher wage levels — plan backwards from March. The O-1 bypasses the lottery for people with a strong record of achievement. Read our H-1B guide and our L-1/O-1/TN guide to understand the mechanics before you build plans around them.

Lane B — The multinational transfer (L-1). Join a multinational's office in your home country, work there at least a year, transfer to the US. No lottery, no cap. Slower but the most predictable path — many professionals choose it deliberately as a two-year plan.

Lane C — US remote employment. Get hired by a US company to work from your country. No visa, no relocation — but the most competitive lane on earth, and most companies pay location-adjusted salaries. Target flat-global-pay employers to maximize earnings.

Lane D — Study, then work. A US degree gives you work authorization (OPT), a US credential, and a local network. Expensive, but statistically the highest-probability route to long-term US employment.

Write your lane choice down. Every later step — which companies you target, what you emphasize, how you network — flows from it.

Step 2: Build a target employer list (not a job-board habit)

Random applications to random postings is how months disappear. Instead, build a list of 30–50 target employers matched to your lane:

  • For sponsorship lanes: use USCIS H-1B employer data to find companies with real petition histories. Employers that have sponsored before will sponsor again — their legal and HR machinery already exists. Prioritize them ruthlessly over companies with no record.
  • For the L-1 lane: list multinationals with substantial operations in your country and the US. Banks, Big Four firms, tech companies, consumer-goods giants, pharma.
  • For remote: list remote-first US companies, prioritizing ones that publish salary bands and (best case) pay flat global rates.
  • For all lanes: filter by industry strength in your occupation — tech workers target tech hubs, finance workers target New York and Charlotte, healthcare workers target large hospital systems.

For each employer, note: do they sponsor (check the data), who is the hiring manager for your function (find them on LinkedIn), and what is their current hiring focus (read their careers page and recent news).

Step 3: Make your application American

US hiring runs on conventions that filter out foreign applicants silently:

  • Resume, not CV: one to two pages, no photo, no date of birth, no personal details, quantified achievement bullets, ATS-friendly formatting. Rebuild yours to the US standard — our US resume format guide walks through it section by section.
  • Keywords mirror the posting: applicant tracking systems filter on exact phrasing. Lift the posting's terms for skills you genuinely have and put them in context inside your bullets.
  • Cover letter: a tailored three-paragraph letter is still expected for competitive roles and is where you address the cross-border question head-on — sponsorship needs, relocation timeline, timezone overlap for remote roles. Our cover letter guide covers the structure.
  • LinkedIn: US recruiters live there. American-format headline with keywords, honest location, recruiter-visible Open-to-Work settings with US target locations, quantified experience entries.

One tailored application beats ten generic ones. Every hiring manager can tell in ten seconds which is which.

Step 4: Network across the time zone gap

Cold applications have low response rates everywhere; from abroad they are worse. Warm introductions change the math:

  • Targeted LinkedIn outreach: 3–5 thoughtful connection requests per day to hiring managers and team members at your target employers. Reference something specific — their team's project, a post they wrote, a shared background. Never pitch in the connection request; start a conversation.
  • Alumni networks: graduates of your university working in the US are the warmest possible contacts. Most people will take a 20-minute call with a fellow alum.
  • Industry communities: Slack groups, Discord servers, and professional associations in your field are where US hiring managers hang out informally. Contribute genuinely for a few weeks before asking anything.
  • Informational interviews: ask for 20 minutes to learn about their team — not for a job. People who like you refer you; referrals bypass the ATS entirely and are the single highest-ROI activity in a from-abroad search.

Time-zone reality: you will take calls at odd hours. Treat 9 PM–midnight your time as normal working hours for this project, and always confirm times in both zones in writing.

Step 5: Interview like you are already there

US interview culture has specific expectations — our interview preparation guide covers the full setup, but the from-abroad essentials:

  • Video setup is non-negotiable: lighting, eye-level camera, headset audio, stable connection with a backup. Technical glitches read as unprofessionalism when the employer is already taking a chance on distance.
  • Answer the unspoken question. Every interviewer is thinking: is this person a risk? Address sponsorship, relocation timeline, and timezone overlap plainly and confidently, early. Hesitation on logistics reads as unpreparedness.
  • Use US answer frameworks: STAR for behavioral questions (Situation, Task, Action, Result — quantified), Present–Past–Future for "tell me about yourself" in 90 seconds.
  • Research US salary bands for the role and metro before any interview — levels.fyi and Glassdoor for tech, plus the pay ranges US employers in many states must now publish in postings. Never anchor to your home-country salary; you will underprice yourself by 30–50%.

Step 6: Negotiate the total package

US offers are packages, not just salaries: base pay, signing bonus, annual bonus, equity/RSUs, 401(k) matching, health insurance, paid time off, and (for international hires) relocation support and visa cost coverage. Negotiate all of it:

  • Do not give a number first if you can avoid it — ask for the budgeted range.
  • Compare net, not gross. State income tax ranges from zero (Texas, Florida, Washington) to over 13% at the top marginal rate (California). A lower headline number in a no-tax state can mean more take-home pay.
  • Get everything in writing. Verbal promises about bonuses, remote flexibility, or relocation support are worth nothing. The written offer is the only thing that counts.
  • Do not accept on the call. Take time to review the full package — employers expect it.

Step 7: Protect yourself from scams

The from-abroad job seeker is the prime target for employment fraud. The rules are simple and absolute:

  • Never pay for a job. Legitimate US employers pay all recruitment and sponsorship costs. Anyone asking you for visa fees, processing charges, or deposits is running a scam.
  • Verify every employer independently — official website, LinkedIn company page with real employees, a call to their publicly listed number. Scammers impersonate real companies.
  • No legitimate process happens entirely over WhatsApp with someone you have never verified.
  • Read our US job scam guide for the full red-flag checklist, including H-1B sponsorship scams and Diversity Visa fraud.

The 90-day action plan

Days 1–14: Foundation. Choose your lane. Rebuild your resume to US format. Fix your LinkedIn. Get your credential evaluation started if needed. Build your 30–50 employer target list.

Days 15–45: Outreach and applications. 2–3 tailored applications per day. 3–5 LinkedIn connection requests per day. Two informational interviews per week. Log everything in a tracker (company, role, date, contact, stage).

Days 46–75: Interviews. By now you should have recruiter screens in motion. Prepare 6–8 STAR stories. Research salary bands for every role before the interview. Keep applying — pipeline matters more than any single opportunity.

Days 76–90: Close. Negotiate offers on total package and net pay. Verify everything. If traction is thin, diagnose: is it the lane (wrong employers?), the application (resume not converting to screens?), or the interview (converting screens but not offers?) — then adjust one variable.

A practical expectation: with 5+ tailored applications a week, first real traction (screens, not offers) typically arrives in 4–8 weeks. Silence before that is normal.

Next steps

  1. Write down your primary lane and your backup lane today — everything else follows.
  2. Pull the USCIS H-1B employer data (or your lane's equivalent) and build the 30–50 employer target list this week.
  3. Rebuild your resume and LinkedIn to US conventions before your first application.
  4. Start the networking engine: 3–5 targeted connection requests a day, two informational interviews a week.
  5. Read the scam guide before you engage with any recruiter who contacts you first.
